# What recovery can achieve {#sec:theory}

I now state which guarantee is achievable under each contract and read model. Proofs are in @sec:proofs. Several of the arguments are short indistinguishability arguments. Their value lies less in any single proposition than in the map they add up to (@tbl:map_theory), which says, for every combination of contract, read model and fault, which guarantee some client-side policy can achieve. Propositions 1–6 and the predictions of the map that the hypotheses test, including where Settle itself must fail, were frozen with the analysis code before any test episode ran. The retention clause of Proposition 1, the corrected statements of Propositions 2, 3, 5 and 6, and Proposition 7 were worked out while writing, after the runs, and I use them to interpret the results rather than as tested predictions. Relative to the frozen map, @tbl:map_theory changes the prevention column in two rows (prevention by waiting is possible for reversible and exclusively written aggregate writes when $\Delta$ is known) and the wording of three others; the convergence predictions that the hypotheses test are unchanged. The frozen version is in the released repository.

**Proposition 1 (without a known in-flight bound, prevention needs a durable key, within its retention).** Suppose the service deduplicates a key for a retention period $r$ after it first records it. Re-issuing with the same key then prevents duplicates if the key is a function of *durable* state and every execution of every attempt falls within $r$ of the first execution. If the number of attempts is capped, this gives at most one execution, and exactly one only if some attempt executes. With a known in-flight bound, a sufficient condition is $t_{\text{last}} - t_1 + \Delta < r$. If the key is regenerated after a client crash, as an in-memory random key would be, two attempts with different keys can both execute, and prevention fails. If an attempt executes later than $r$ after the first execution, the service no longer recognises the key, and the operation is protected only by whatever attribution the contract offers apart from the key. Retention that ends with a state change rather than after a time, such as a deduplication key that lasts while an alert is open, behaves in the same way, with the state change in place of $r$. Without a key, no policy that acts only on observations prevents duplicates under late commits when $\Delta$ is unknown [@li2026limbo].

The retention condition matters in practice. A key that expires after one hour does not protect an agent that resumes after a crash the next morning, or one that waits for a human approval before retrying. The census (@sec:census) finds documented retentions from one hour to at least thirty days, alongside conditional creates whose protection lasts as long as the object exists.

**Proposition 2 (convergence for attributable, reversible writes).** Assume the following:

(a) the tag $\tau$ of $\ell$ can be recovered after a crash, for example from an intent record written before the first dispatch;
(b) every executed attempt persists $\tau$ unchanged;
(c) the read path is complete and sound for $\tau$ after lag $\lambda$;
(d) the in-flight bound $\Delta$ holds;
(e) a targeted compensation exists, and it is idempotent or itself keyed or tagged, so that it can be retried through the faulty channel;
(f) no other actor neutralises effects that carry $\tau$;
(g) tags are unique per operation, and no other actor writes $\tau$ into an effect.

Consider the following policy, which proceeds in rounds. In each round it waits until $t_{\text{last}} + \Delta + \lambda$ and counts $N$, the effects that carry $\tau$. If $N = 0$, it re-issues with the same $\tau$ and starts a new round. If $N > 1$, it compensates $N-1$ effects, chosen by id. The state is then consistent from the end of the final round on. The number of rounds is finite with probability one if each attempt executes with positive probability, and a policy that caps the number of rounds, as Settle does, reports *unknown* when the cap is reached. If (d) is violated and delays follow a distribution $F$, the round that ends at horizon $H$ leaves an inconsistent state only if some attempt executes after $H - \lambda$. If attempts execute independently of each other, that happens with probability at most $\sum_i p_i\,\big(1 - F(H - \lambda - t_i)\big)$, summed over the attempts $i$ dispatched at $t_i$, where $p_i$ is the probability that attempt $i$ executes at all; this is a statement about the round as a whole, not conditional on what the round observed. The policy needs no assumption about the agent or about the order of faults.

**Proposition 3 (irreversible writes: convergence collapses to prevention).** If an effect cannot be neutralised, convergence requires that $\ell$ never executed twice, so convergence equals prevention, and Proposition 1 applies. With a known $\Delta$, the policy "wait $\Delta + \lambda$, count by $\tau$, re-issue only if $N = 0$" prevents every duplicate that the *client* could cause. The tag is what makes the test $N = 0$ exact; Proposition 4 shows that a test by content is not exact. No client policy, however, prevents a duplicate that the *transport* creates by redelivering a request the client issued once. For irreversible writes without a key, a redelivered request is therefore an unrecoverable duplicate, and the best a client can do is to detect it by its tag and report it. The same wait-then-count policy would also prevent duplicates of reversible writes. Settle does not use it for them, because convergence lets it replace the wait by compensation.

**Proposition 4 (content verification is unsound under look-alikes).** Consider a world $w_1$ in which the client’s attempt executed and no look-alike exists, and a world $w_2$ in which the client’s attempt did not execute and another actor created an effect with identical content. Under a content read model the two worlds are observation-equivalent, while $N_\ell$ differs. Every content-only policy is therefore wrong in one of them. A policy that skips the write leaves $\ell$ missing in $w_2$. A policy that re-issues duplicates $\ell$ in $w_1$. And a policy that re-issues and then removes one of the two matching records either removes the other actor's effect in $w_2$ or removes its own new effect, which leaves $\ell$ missing in $w_2$. A client tag restores attributability, because look-alikes do not carry $\tau$. Fields that real read models often expose, such as the author account and the creation time, separate the two worlds only when the other actor uses a different account or writes at a distinguishable time; they are a partial substitute for a tag, not a guarantee. The follow-up study (@sec:followup) measures both cases.

**Proposition 5 (aggregates: exclusivity is the only general route).** Under an aggregate read model, $\ell$ is attributable if no other actor writes to the aggregate during the ambiguity window. If other actors may write the same unit, or units of unknown size, then a world in which the client’s attempt executed and theirs did not gives the same aggregate as a world in which theirs executed and the client’s did not. Neither prevention nor convergence is then achievable without a key, and fungible compensation does not help, because the number of surplus units is not identifiable. The only correct behaviour is safe abstention. (Concurrent writes of known and distinct sizes would keep the count identifiable. My shared-aggregate worlds, like most real counters, do not have that property.)

**Proposition 6 (the tag must survive a crash).** Suppose the client can crash after dispatching an attempt and before recording its outcome. After the restart, the client must issue $\ell$ if it was never dispatched and must not issue it blindly if it was. If the persisted state does not show whether $\ell$ may have been dispatched, and the tag of a possible dispatch cannot be recovered, the two cases are observation-equivalent to the client, which then either duplicates or omits $\ell$. Two kinds of durable state suffice to decide correctly: a write-ahead intent record that carries the tag, or a tag derived deterministically from durable step identity, provided every unfinished step is treated after a restart as possibly dispatched and resolved by waiting and counting. A durable step checkpoint *without* a recoverable tag, which is what my drivers keep and what the S−j ablation is left with, is not enough: it shows that the step may have been dispatched, but leaves the client nothing to count by except content, which Proposition 4 shows to be unsound. Workflow engines persist the *scheduling* of an activity durably but execute the activity at least once [@doc_temporal]. Their durable identity is therefore available, but unless it is written into the effect it does not make the effect attributable: durability without attribution yields at-least-once effects. Likewise, an agent framework that replays a pending tool call after a restart turns every crash during a write into a blind re-issue.

**Proposition 7 (a policy that stops must bet).** Let $\ell$ be attributable, and let $t_d$ be the time of the policy's last action on $\ell$, after which it neither observes nor acts on $\ell$. For a reversible write $t_d$ is the final reconciliation; for an irreversible write it is the decision whether to re-issue. Suppose an attempt dispatched at $t_0$ is still unresolved at $t_d$. Let $w_0$ be the worlds in which it never executes, and $w_L$ those in which it executes after $t_d - \lambda$, too late to be seen by the last read. Up to $t_d$ the two kinds of world are observation-equivalent. At $t_d$, the policy decides how many of its *other* effects of $\ell$ remain standing: none, by not re-issuing or by compensating its re-issue, or one, by re-issuing or keeping the re-issue. The final count is that number plus one in $w_L$, so every choice is wrong in either $w_0$ or $w_L$. Hence, for every policy $\pi$ and every history $h$ that satisfies the premise,
$$\Pr[\pi \text{ inconsistent} \mid h] \;\ge\; \min\{\Pr[w_0 \mid h],\; \Pr[w_L \mid h]\}.$$
Settle always keeps one effect standing, so its error at such a history is $\Pr[w_L \mid h]$, plus $\Pr[w_0 \mid h]$ times the probability that its own re-issue fails to execute. It attains the bound exactly when its re-issue executes and a late landing is no more likely, given the history, than an attempt that never executes.

Proposition 7 makes two points about the residual failures I measure. First, they are the price of a bet that every stopping policy must make, not a defect of Settle's rule: a policy that bet the other way would fail in the worlds where the request was lost instead. Settle bets on the lost request because then the operation is complete and the error, if any, is a surplus effect rather than a missing one. In a deployment where late commits after the horizon are more likely than lost requests, the opposite bet has the lower error. My late-tail worlds are designed so that every request eventually commits, so there the opposite bet would have been right. Second, at a fixed decision time no rule does better than the bound, so the residual risk falls only if $t_d$ moves later or if the posterior shifts. For an irreversible write, a later $t_d$ means a longer wait before the re-issue, which the agent experiences as latency. For a reversible write the reconciliation can instead continue *after* the agent has reported, because compensation needs no agent. @sec:horizon measures the trade-off.

The experiments fill each row with measured rates (@tbl:map). {#tbl:map_theory}

@tbl:map_theory summarises the propositions and yields three predictions that the experiments test. Idempotency keys and workflow engines should match Settle on class-K writes and fall short on class-A writes. Every method that recognises its own effects by content should fail under look-alikes. And no method should succeed in the last row; the best a method can do there is to say so.
