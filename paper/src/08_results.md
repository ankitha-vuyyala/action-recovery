# Results {#sec:results}

## The test runs {#sec:runs}

The test split comprises {{n.scripted|int}} scripted and {{n.llm|int}} language-model episodes. Every scheduled episode finished, so no record is excluded. The language-model test runs cost {{cost.test|usd}}, and the main study as a whole, including its pilot and development checks, cost {{cost.total|usd}}. With the follow-up study ({{fu.usd.new|usd}}, @sec:followup), all model calls of the project cost {{fu.usd.ledger|usd}}. Two amendments were made after the freeze (@sec:prereg). The first fixed a lock race in the cost ledger during the runs and could not change any outcome. The second fixed a defect found in the audit before writing: transfer keys chosen by the models collided across episodes that replay the same task, so the ledger refused the transfer while the tool reported success. All affected language-model episodes were re-run under the amended code. The verdicts of H1–H4 and H6 did not change, H5's cost criterion for `gpt-5.4-nano` moved from inconclusive to confirmed, and @sec:llmresults also reports the numbers from before the amendment. Every condition left a consistent final state in every one of the {{free.n|int}} fault-free scripted episodes, so the harness and the grader create no inconsistency of their own.

Plain retries, client retries, in-memory keys and the workflow engine all end at about the same level: {{h1.C0.rate|pct}}, {{h1.C2.rate|pct}}, {{h1.C3.rate|pct}} and {{h1.C4.rate|pct}}. Rollback is the worst condition at {{h1.C8.rate|pct}}: it compensates and restarts on every error, including errors after which the write *had* taken effect, so the restarted workflow duplicates it. The oracle knows every hidden outcome but cannot remove a duplicate, and ends at {{h1.O.rate|pct}}. All its failures in this population are transport redeliveries, which no knowledge of outcomes prevents and only compensation repairs. This is why Settle does better than the oracle in this population; without redelivery and partial batches the oracle is perfect (@tbl:h1modes).

**Where the difference comes from.** A pooled rate over a designed grid depends on the mix of fault modes, so @tbl:h1modes breaks the H1 population down by mode and adds the ablations. Three of them (S−h, S−c, S−j) use tags but lack another component; S−t keeps every component but matches by content. Two modes favour Settle by construction: every condition without a final sweep fails on redelivery, and every condition that does not check rows individually fails on a partial batch. Without these two modes, Settle leaves {{rb.core.S|pct}} of {{rb.core_n|int}} worlds inconsistent against {{rb.core.Gw|pct}} for the waiting guard, and the oracle is perfect. The ablations separate the components. A tag with a journal and compensation but without the horizon (S−h) reaches {{rb.core.S-h|pct}}, about the level of the waiting guard. A tag with a horizon but without compensation (S−c) reaches {{rb.core.S-c|pct}}, and a tag that is lost in a crash (S−j) reaches {{rb.core.S-j|pct}}. Attribution is therefore necessary but not sufficient: a tag-aware variant that lacks the horizon or compensation is not much better than the waiting guard, as Proposition 2 leads one to expect. These ablations remove a component from Settle; they are not the strongest tag-based alternative. Proposition 3 implies that a tag-based *wait-then-count* policy, which waits out $\Delta$ before every re-issue and never compensates, prevents duplicates whenever $\Delta$ holds. The follow-up study (@sec:followup) runs exactly this policy and finds it identical to Settle on every ordinary fault, so Settle's advantage over it lies mainly in transport redelivery. The comparison with the waiting guard does not depend on the clustering. Resampling the eight templates instead of the 24 template × instance clusters gives a paired difference of {{rb.tclust_h1.diff|pp}} (95% CI {{rb.tclust_h1.lo|pp}} to {{rb.tclust_h1.hi|pp}}), or {{rb.tclust_core.diff|pp}} ({{rb.tclust_core.lo|pp}} to {{rb.tclust_core.hi|pp}}) without redelivery and partial batches, and Settle is better than the waiting guard within every one of the eight templates (a sign test over templates gives $p$ = 0.008, two-sided).

## Where keys and workflow engines are enough, and where they are not (H3) {#sec:h3}

@tbl:map fills in the achievability map with measured rates. On class-K writes, where the service honours a key, Settle and the workflow engine with durable keys are both exact: neither leaves any of the {{h3.pairs|int}} paired worlds inconsistent, so the equivalence margin is met trivially. H3 is {{h3.verdict}}. The oracle's three failures in this cell (2.8% of the 108 keyed worlds) are late-tail worlds in which the delayed TigerBeetle transfer, injected to commit after 50–84 s, never executed; conditions that re-issued with the same id ended consistent there. This is a fault that did not realise its intended outcome, and it affects only the oracle, which by design never re-issues a write it expects to land. Keys generated in memory (C3) fail in exactly the crash worlds ({{h3.C3.crash|pct}} inconsistent) and nowhere else ({{h3.C3.rest|pct}}), as Proposition 1 predicts.

On key-less writes the picture reverses. Keys and the workflow engine are no better than plain retries, because there is nothing to send the key to. Over all key-less ambiguous worlds their rates are {{keyless.C3|pct}} and {{keyless.C4|pct}}, against {{keyless.C0|pct}} for plain retries. The workflow engine's durable history does not stop a crashed step from taking effect twice: in key-less crash-after-dispatch worlds it is inconsistent in {{crash_keyless.C4|pct}} of episodes, against {{crash_keyless.S|pct}} for Settle, because resuming the step means executing the activity again (Proposition 6). In every key-less cell Settle has the lowest rate of the non-oracle conditions or ties for it, and its remaining failures in the attributable, reversible cells are all delays beyond $\Delta$.

## Content-based verification under look-alikes (H2) {#sec:h2}

When another user of the same account creates a look-alike, every method that recognises its own effects by content fails in more than half of the 252 ambiguous look-alike worlds of all contract classes (@tbl:map restricts them to class-A reversible writes, 168 worlds): verify-before-retry in {{h2.C5.rate|pct}}, the guard in {{h2.C6.rate|pct}}, and Settle without tags in {{h2.S-t.rate|pct}}. When the agent's write never executed, these methods take the other user's record for their own and skip the write, so their own effect is missing. Settle's rate is {{h2.S.rate|pct}}, and H2 is {{h2.verdict}}. Rollback is the only baseline that deletes, and it causes collateral damage in {{h2.C8.collateral|pct}} of these worlds. Every case is a world whose focal write was a comment: after the comment failed, rollback compensated the completed steps by deleting the agent's own issue, and with it the other user's comment on that issue. Compensation that is correct for the agent's own effect still destroys what others attached to it.

## What no method can do, and what Settle does instead (H4) {#sec:h4}

When another writer adjusts the same counter or stock level, every non-oracle condition is inconsistent in at least {{h4.agg_min|pct}} of ambiguous worlds (H4a {{h4.verdict_a}}). Settle abstains there: it returns *unknown* instead of guessing. Across shared-aggregate and irreversible worlds, Settle overclaims in {{h4.S_overclaim|pct}} of its episodes ({{h4b.over|int}} of {{h4b.n|int}}; H4b {{h4.verdict_b}}), and it reports a problem in {{h4b.reported|int}} of the {{h4b.inc|int}} episodes that ended inconsistent. Plain retries, by comparison, overclaim in {{amb.C0.overclaim|pct}} of all ambiguous worlds. In irreversible worlds every condition, the oracle included, shows duplicates or missing effects (H4c {{h4.verdict_c}}). H4a and H4c, like the lenient thresholds of H5 below, confirm properties that the design of the fault grid makes very likely: half of each shared-aggregate pair is a never-executed world, and redelivery of an irreversible write cannot be undone. They check that the harness behaves as the theory says rather than testing Settle.

**Every one of Settle's failures is accounted for by the theory.** Of Settle's {{sc.S_amb_n|int}} ambiguous scripted episodes, {{sc.S_fail|int}} ended inconsistent (@tbl:sfail). Each is either a violation of the in-flight bound that Settle assumes, whose consequence Propositions 2 and 7 bound, or falls into a cell where the theory admits no client-side guarantee. The first kind is not a prediction in the strong sense; it is a failure of an assumption, and the theory says how likely it is.

The late-tail worlds test the qualitative prediction of Proposition 2 that Settle can fail only when the in-flight bound is violated. Of {{sc.lt.n|int}} late-tail worlds, {{sc.lt.over|int}} had a delay above the assumed bound of 20 s. Of the twelve worlds above the bound in which Settle did not fail, seven had a keyed focal write, which the bound does not affect, and in the other five the late commit still landed before Settle's final sweep at the end of the episode. Within the late-tail worlds Settle therefore fails only when its assumed bound is wrong ({{sc.lt.fail_over|int}} of the {{sc.lt.over|int}} worlds above the bound, none of the others). ## How the remaining risk trades against latency {#sec:horizon}

Settle has one parameter that the theory does not fix, the assumed in-flight bound $\Delta$. A larger bound buys a lower residual risk at the cost of latency, and Proposition 7 says that no smarter rule escapes this trade-off at a fixed decision time. This analysis is exploratory. To trace the trade-off without new runs, I use a property of Settle's decision rule that the recorded episodes let me check. In a late-commit world, Settle ends inconsistent if and only if the commit lands after its last decision about the operation. For an irreversible write that decision is taken $\Delta$ after the last attempt, which in these worlds is the first; for a reversible one it is the final sweep at the end of the episode. At the frozen $\Delta$ = 20 s, this rule reproduces the measured outcome in {{hz.agree|int}} of the {{hz.n_timing|int}} recorded late and late-tail worlds with exact reads, which is the population I replay. (It does not apply to shared-aggregate worlds, where Settle abstains.) Each recorded world therefore also tells me its outcome under a different bound: the decision time shifts by the difference in $\Delta$, while the recorded delay of the commit stays the same. The replay assumes that Settle's other actions do not change with $\Delta$, which holds for its decision rule but was verified only at the frozen value.

![Exploratory replay of the recorded Settle episodes under other assumed in-flight bounds $\Delta$ (scripted driver). Left: inconsistent final states in the late-commit worlds with exact reads, for the fixed 12 s delay and the heavy-tailed 5–120 s delay. Right: median duration of Settle episodes in the lost-reply, timeout and late-commit modes. At the frozen $\Delta$ = 20 s the replay reproduces the measured outcome in all but one of these 168 worlds.](fig_horizon.png){#fig:horizon width=80%}

@fig:horizon shows the result. With the fixed 12 s delay, a bound shorter than the delay is a cliff: at $\Delta$ = 10 s, {{hz.10.late12|pct0}} of these worlds end inconsistent, and from $\Delta$ = 12.5 s on none does. With the heavy-tailed delay the trade-off is gradual. The replayed inconsistency falls from {{hz.10.tail|pct0}} at 10 s through {{hz.20.tail|pct0}} at 20 s (measured: {{sc.lt.fail_over|int}} of {{sc.lt.n|int}}, one world more than the replay) and {{hz.60.tail|pct0}} at 60 s to {{hz.120.tail|pct0}} at 120 s, the upper end of the distribution I chose. Over the same range the median duration of an episode in the lost-reply, timeout and late-commit modes grows from {{hz.10.lat|s}} to {{hz.120.lat|s}}, against {{hz.c0_latency|s}} for plain retries; only the episodes that actually waited out the bound move with it. The curve is the tail of the delay distribution seen through Settle's decision times, exactly as Propositions 2 and 7 predict. Two consequences follow. A deployment should set $\Delta$ from the delay tail of its own services. Settle's journal records every late commit that a sweep catches, but those observations are censored at the horizon, so the part of the tail that matters most is visible only if reconciliation continues beyond it. And since the horizon of a reversible write can continue after the agent has reported, the latency cost needs to be paid in full only for irreversible writes (@sec:discussion).

## How language-model agents treat an ambiguous outcome {#sec:rq1}

Without a recovery layer, the three models split into two behaviours after their first failed or timed-out write (@tbl:rq1, @fig:rq1). `gpt-4.1-mini` and `gpt-5.4-nano` re-issue the write immediately in {{rq1.gpt-4.1-mini.reissue|pct0}} and {{rq1.gpt-5.4-nano.reissue|pct0}} of these episodes. `gpt-5.6-luna`, the only model that reasons by default, re-issues at once in only {{rq1.gpt-5.6-luna.reissue|pct0}}, and its first action is a read in {{rq1.gpt-5.6-luna.check|pct0}}. The hidden truth changes what the models do only modestly. The paired worlds give identical tool observations, so the remaining differences reflect sampling and the small differences between the episodes' histories, such as ids and timings, rather than information about the outcome. The immediate re-issue rate is {{rq1.gpt-5.6-luna.reissue.committed|pct0}} in committed and {{rq1.gpt-5.6-luna.reissue.not_executed|pct0}} in never-executed worlds for `gpt-5.6-luna`, {{rq1.gpt-4.1-mini.reissue.committed|pct0}} and {{rq1.gpt-4.1-mini.reissue.not_executed|pct0}} for `gpt-4.1-mini`, and {{rq1.gpt-5.4-nano.reissue.committed|pct0}} and {{rq1.gpt-5.4-nano.reissue.not_executed|pct0}} for `gpt-5.4-nano`.

Checking first helps, but only as far as Propositions 4 and 6 allow. Without a layer, `gpt-5.6-luna` ends inconsistent in {{rq1.gpt-5.6-luna.inconsistent|pct0}} of these episodes, against {{rq1.gpt-4.1-mini.inconsistent|pct0}} for `gpt-4.1-mini`, and its failures concentrate exactly where checking cannot work. In look-alike worlds ({{cell.gpt-5.6-luna.C0.lookalike|pct0}} inconsistent) the other user's identical record convinces it that its own write took effect. In crash worlds ({{cell.gpt-5.6-luna.C0.crash_after|pct0}}) the framework replays the pending call after the restart, before the model can intervene. Under heavy-tailed delays ({{cell.gpt-5.6-luna.C0.late_tail|pct0}}) the record is not there yet when the model looks. With exact reads and a short delay, the same model fails in only {{cell.gpt-5.6-luna.C0.lost_ack|pct0}} of the worlds with a lost reply. In the look-alike and late-commit cells the model's observation is insufficient: a more capable model would read the same look-alike and the same empty result, unless it used fields my look-alikes do not distinguish, such as author and creation time, or waited longer than my late commits. Crash replay is different: it is a framework behaviour that no model can influence.

Asking the model to reflect on the error (C7) gave no benefit in hard worlds: it was two to six points worse than the plain agent for every model (@tbl:llm). In lost-reply worlds with exact reads it moved `gpt-4.1-mini` from {{cell.gpt-4.1-mini.C0.lost_ack|pct0}} to {{cell.gpt-4.1-mini.C7.lost_ack|pct0}} and `gpt-5.4-nano` from {{cell.gpt-5.4-nano.C0.lost_ack|pct0}} to {{cell.gpt-5.4-nano.C7.lost_ack|pct0}}, but with fifteen episodes per cell these differences are within sampling noise. Reports were often not honest about the uncertainty. After an ambiguous write, `gpt-4.1-mini` listed an operation as uncertain in {{rq1.gpt-4.1-mini.listed_uncertain|pct0}} of episodes and claimed completion in {{rq1.gpt-4.1-mini.claimed_completed|pct0}}; `gpt-5.6-luna` listed one in {{rq1.gpt-5.6-luna.listed_uncertain|pct0}} and claimed completion in {{rq1.gpt-5.6-luna.claimed_completed|pct0}}; `gpt-5.4-nano` listed one in {{rq1.gpt-5.4-nano.listed_uncertain|pct0}}.

The main runs bound writes to intents through the harness's step mapping (@sec:method); the follow-up repeats them with plan-free binding and gets identical outcomes, and adds a frontier model (@sec:followup). H6 was confirmed for both models before and after the amendment, and every number reported here is the corrected one.

## Cost (H5) {#sec:cost}

Settle's cost is latency, not model calls. In fault-free worlds it adds {{h5.free.diff|f2}} s per scripted episode relative to the guard (95% upper bound {{h5.free.hi|f2}} s). In ambiguous worlds it adds {{h5.amb.diff|f1}} s on average, almost all of it the reconciliation horizon, whose trade-off @sec:horizon traces. Settle *saves* model calls, because the agent rarely sees an error it has to reason about. Its cost per episode relative to the guard is {{h5.cost.gpt-5.6-luna.ratio|f2}} for `gpt-5.6-luna` (95% upper bound {{h5.cost.gpt-5.6-luna.hi|f2}}), {{h5.cost.gpt-4.1-mini.ratio|f2}} for `gpt-4.1-mini` ({{h5.cost.gpt-4.1-mini.hi|f2}}) and {{h5.cost.gpt-5.4-nano.ratio|f2}} for `gpt-5.4-nano` ({{h5.cost.gpt-5.4-nano.hi|f2}}), against the pre-registered limit of 1.25; the cost criterion is {{h5.cost.gpt-5.6-luna.verdict}} for all three, and the latency criterion is {{h5.latency_verdict}}.
