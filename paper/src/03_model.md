# Problem setting {#sec:model}

**Operations, attempts and effects.** A *logical operation* $\ell$ is one intended change to an external service: create one issue, send one e-mail, make one transfer. The client makes *attempts* $a_1, a_2, \dots$ of $\ell$, dispatched at times $t_1 < t_2 < \dots$. An attempt either *executes* at an unknown time $t_i + \delta_i$ with $\delta_i \ge 0$, or never executes; the transport may also *redeliver* an attempt, so that it executes a second time although the client issued it once. An executed attempt creates one *effect* $e$, which is *standing* until it is neutralised. For every attempt the client receives an acknowledgement, an error, a timeout or nothing, and it may issue reads through the read paths that the service offers. The client can crash, and only what it wrote to durable storage survives a crash. Other actors share the service and create effects of their own.

A *world* $w$ fixes, for every attempt, whether, when and how often it executes, together with the other actors' effects. Worlds are drawn from a prior that describes the deployment, such as how often requests are lost, how delays are distributed and how active other users are. The client's *history* $h$ is the sequence of its own actions, the responses and the read results, and $\Pr[\cdot \mid h]$ denotes the posterior over worlds. A *policy* maps histories to actions: re-issue, read, wait, neutralise an effect, or report. Two worlds are *observation-equivalent up to time $t$* if every policy receives the same history in both until $t$.

**Contract properties of a write.** Three properties belong to the service's interface, not to the agent. The first is a *native key* (class K): attempts that carry the same key create at most one effect, and a repeat returns the original outcome or an "exists" refusal. Real keys come with a *retention* $r$, after which the service forgets the key and treats a repeat as new. The second is the *read model*, which says what reads reveal about standing effects. Under an *exact* read model, reads return all standing effects with their fields. A *lagged* read model is exact, but only for effects older than a lag $\lambda$. Under a *content* read model, effects are found by their content, and other actors may create effects with identical content, which I call *look-alikes*. An *aggregate* read model returns only a count or a sum. The third property is *reversibility*. A *targeted* compensation $c(e)$ neutralises exactly the effect $e$, such as deleting issue #$n$ or row $r$. A *fungible* compensation neutralises one unit of an aggregate, such as a decrement. Some effects cannot be neutralised at all; a delivered e-mail is the standard example.

**In-flight bound.** An attempt that has not executed within $\Delta$ of its dispatch never executes. $\Delta$ may be unknown to the client, or the delay distribution $F$ may have a heavy tail, in which case any finite assumed bound is sometimes wrong.

**Attributability.** The central notion is the following.

> **Definition 1 (Attributability).** Under a read model with lag $\lambda$, operation $\ell$ is *attributable* if, for every time $t$, the number $N_\ell(t-\lambda)$ of standing effects of $\ell$ that executed before $t-\lambda$ is a function of the client's history at $t$. Equivalently, no two worlds that are observation-equivalent up to $t$ disagree on $N_\ell(t-\lambda)$.

Attributability concerns effects that already exist. It does not make the *eventual* count identifiable, because an attempt may still be in flight. That additionally requires the in-flight bound: once every attempt is older than $\Delta$, the eventual count equals the attributable count. The theory below separates the two on purpose, because attribution is a property of the API, whereas the in-flight bound is a property of the network and the service's queues.

Five mechanisms make an operation attributable (@tbl:attrib). A native key bounds $N_\ell$ by one and answers queries for it. A *client tag*, an operation id $\tau$ written into a field that the service persists and returns, lets a read count exactly the effects that carry $\tau$, provided tags are unique per operation and no one else writes $\tau$ into an effect. *Exclusivity*, being the only writer to an aggregate, makes the change in the aggregate equal to $N_\ell$. If no one else writes with the client's account and the service records authorship, the account itself identifies the client's effects, as the follow-up study measures (@sec:followup). Finally, an id returned by the service identifies the effect, but it is lost exactly when the reply is lost, which is the case that matters.

| Source | Why $N_\ell$ becomes identifiable | Example in my stack |
|---|---|---|
| Native key | the service enforces $N_\ell \le 1$ and answers "exists" | TigerBeetle transfer id; S3 conditional create |
| Client tag | every effect carries $\tau$; a read returns all effects with $\tau$ | Gitea issue body, mail Message-ID, row column, stream field |
| Exclusivity | only the client writes, so the aggregate's change equals $N_\ell$ | a counter with no other writer |
| Own account | only the client writes with its account, and the service records the author | Gitea issues and comments when other users use other accounts |
| Returned id | the reply names the new effect | lost together with the reply |

**Guarantees.** I distinguish three guarantees. *Prevention* means that $\ell$ executes exactly once over the episode; it is the target of keys and of prior agent benchmarks [@li2026limbo]. *Convergence at horizon $H$* means that from time $H$ on exactly one effect of $\ell$ stands, including after every in-flight attempt has landed, and that no effect of another operation or actor has been neutralised; a transient duplicate is allowed if it is removed by $H$. *Safe abstention* means that, where neither guarantee is achievable, the client reports $\ell$ as unknown and never claims completion; it is a property of the report, not of the state. Prevention implies convergence once the single execution has landed, provided no other actor's effect was touched; convergence does not imply prevention. My thesis is that convergence is the right target wherever prevention is provably impossible and effects are attributable and reversible.

**Primary metric.** The primary outcome of every episode is therefore *residual inconsistency at quiescence*. After the client finishes and every in-flight request has landed, an episode is inconsistent if a required effect is missing, if a surplus effect stands, or if another actor's effect was removed. I also report duplicate executions including compensated ones, which is the prevention metric of prior work; irreversible duplicates; missing effects; collateral damage; and *overclaim*, an inconsistent final state with nothing reported as uncertain.
