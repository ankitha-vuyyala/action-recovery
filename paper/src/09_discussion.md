# Discussion {#sec:discussion}

**Where idempotency keys and workflow engines fall short.** Keys and durable execution answer the question "has this *request* been processed?" The question an agent faces after an ambiguous outcome is a different one: "how many *effects* of this operation exist?" The two coincide only when the service enforces the key, and the census shows that this is the exception outside payments. Without a key on the service, a key the client generates is a string the service ignores, however durable it is, and the effect it should protect can still be duplicated by a late commit, a redelivery or a replayed call. A workflow engine's durable history makes a crashed step resume, but resuming means executing the activity again: the engine provides at-least-once *execution* by design [@doc_temporal; @doc_dbos], and with key-less tools that becomes at-least-once *effects*. My measurements locate this boundary. In-memory keys and the workflow engine left {{keyless.C3|pct}} and {{keyless.C4|pct}} of ambiguous key-less episodes inconsistent, no different from plain retries ({{keyless.C0|pct}}). On keyed writes the engine matched Settle exactly. And on key-less writes its durable history did not stop a crashed step from taking effect twice, in {{crash_keyless.C4|pct}} of crash-after-dispatch worlds. Even where a key exists, its retention bounds the protection: a key that the service forgets after an hour does not cover an agent that resumes the next day.

**What API designers can do.** The most useful thing a key-less API can offer an agent is not necessarily a key, but a persisted, queryable, client-controlled field. A metadata map, an external id or a header that the service stores and lets clients look up exactly is enough to build attribution and convergence on the client (Proposition 2). The census shows that many APIs already have such a field and that the gaps are specific. Some APIs persist the field but cannot look it up, such as Pub/Sub attributes or SQS message attributes. Others look it up only through a search documented as lagging by an unstated amount, which weakens assumption (c). Still others return only a count or a sum, which removes attribution entirely (Proposition 5). The common design in which the server returns the new id is no help in the case that matters, since the id is lost together with the reply. Tool annotations such as idempotency hints [@doc_mcp] describe intent but cannot provide either property. A tool description that says how to *find* an effect again would provide it, and the tag can usually live in a field that end users never see: {{cen.tag.hidden|int}} of the taggable key-less APIs in the census document such a field.

**When a tag is needed.** Proposition 4 is an indistinguishability argument, and the follow-up shows where it binds in practice. A look-alike created by a *different* account was harmless to a content check that also filtered by author and waited out the bound; such a check matched Settle. The tag is needed when the look-alike can come from the same account (another instance of the same agent, or another worker sharing its service account, which is common), and on services that record no author, such as the mail, rows and stream events of my stack. Since one cannot usually rule out the first case, the tag remains the robust choice, but the claim is narrower than "content checks fail under look-alikes"; how often real services lack an author field is something the census did not code.

**What agent frameworks can do.** Two framework behaviours caused failures in my language-model runs. Replaying a pending tool call after a restart turns every crash during a write into a blind re-issue (Proposition 6). Leaving clean-up to the model is the second: under look-alikes, a careful model removed what it took to be its own duplicate and deleted someone else's record. The model was not careless; by content, the two records were indistinguishable. Both behaviours are fixed by two mechanisms that need no better model: a tag that survives the restart, for instance in a journal written before dispatch, which makes a restart resolve the outcome instead of repeating the call, and attribution, which gates every destructive action.

**Prevention, convergence and what each costs.** The follow-up study (@sec:followup) sharpens the choice between the two guarantees. A tag-based wait-then-count policy, which is prevention without a key, was exactly as good as Settle on lost replies, requests that never arrived, held connections, bounded delays, crashes and partial batches, and about 3 s faster per episode of the H1 population. Convergence earned its place for duplicates created by the transport, which no client-side prevention can stop; its small advantage under long delays came from four worlds just past the bound and reflects decision timing rather than a robust difference. A deployment that knows its transport never redelivers and its delays are bounded can therefore use the simpler policy. One that cannot should converge. If late transport duplicates are possible it should also sweep in every episode, at a cost of about one in-flight bound of latency per episode, because a sweep that runs only after visible trouble misses every duplicate that arrives after the agent has finished; the main study's latency criterion would rule this out, so the choice is a real trade-off.

**Convergence is not prevention, and latency is negotiable.** Settle allows transient duplicates and removes them by the horizon. That is acceptable for an issue, a row or an event, but not for effects that others react to before compensation, such as a page that wakes someone or a webhook that triggers a shipment. For irreversible effects the theory and my measurements agree that convergence collapses to prevention. Settle then waits out the in-flight bound before re-issuing, and reports an unrecoverable duplicate instead of hiding it. Where even a transient duplicate is harmful, the right tool is a key at the service, and my results quantify what is lost without one. For reversible effects the latency cost is not intrinsic. Proposition 7 shows that the residual risk falls only as the last decision moves later, but nothing requires the agent to wait for that decision: the journal outlives the episode, and a background reconciler can keep counting effects by tag and compensate a late surplus after the agent has reported. My implementation blocks until the horizon and I did not evaluate this asynchronous variant. In my late-tail worlds it could have removed at most the {{rb.lt_fail_rev|int}} delay failures on reversible writes; the other {{rb.lt_fail_irr|int}} concern irreversible writes, for which only a longer wait before the re-issue helps.

## Limitations and threats to validity {#sec:limits}

**External validity of the stack.** The services are real, but they run on one machine under synthetic load, and the background actor is the only other user. Real deployments have more concurrent writers, but I have no data on how often they produce look-alikes as close as mine. In the main study the background actor uses the same account and writes at the moment of the fault, which is the worst case for checks by content; the follow-up adds look-alikes from a second account. The census establishes how real contracts are distributed over the classes of the theory, but it codes documented behaviour, not behaviour under fault. The two together argue that the classes I test are the ones real services fall into; they do not show how often each fault occurs in production. Production traces of ambiguous outcomes would close this gap, and I know of no public source of them.

**Faults.** Faults are injected at the network and process level and validated per service against the service's own records (@sec:validation). Real outages produce mixtures of faults and delays longer than my twelve-second late commit. The heavy-tailed mode and the replay in @sec:horizon show how the residual risk grows when the in-flight bound is violated, as Propositions 2 and 7 predict, but my delay distribution is a choice, not a measurement. The replay itself is exploratory: it relies on Settle's decision rule, which it verified on 167 of the 168 recorded late-commit worlds with exact reads, and not on new runs at each bound.

**Models.** I test four OpenAI models at their default settings, all from one provider: three smaller models in the main study and the frontier model `gpt-5.6-sol` in the follow-up, which ran one repeat and two conditions within a fixed budget. The frontier model behaved like the most careful smaller model, checking state first in most episodes and failing in the same cells. Models from other providers, other reasoning settings and agent frameworks that do not replay pending calls remain untested. A model that compares authors and creation times, or waits longer, might do better against less adversarial look-alikes and shorter delays than mine.

**Baselines.** The guard [@li2026limbo], verify-before-retry [@mansoor2026verified] and rollback [@perera2026rac] were re-implemented from their published descriptions and may differ from their authors' code in detail. I describe what each re-implementation does (@sec:conditions), use the strongest configuration of each family I could build without attribution, and release the code. Settle, the theory and the harness were all designed by me. The frozen predictions, the matched-latency control, the per-mode breakdown and the tag-aware ablations limit how much this can favour Settle, but they do not replace an independent replication. The baselines of the main study are denied tags by design, so part of the headline comparison confirms Proposition 4 rather than testing an engineering choice. The follow-up adds the two fairest competitors I could construct, a tag-based wait-then-count policy and a waiting guard with an author filter, and reports where each matches Settle.

**Pre-registration.** The freezes are internal: they consist of source hashes recorded in the repository, not of a registration with a third party, and the freeze, runs and writing took place within a few days. The follow-up study has its own plan and freeze, written after review of the main results and before any follow-up episode; its analysis script needed one correction after the runs (@sec:prereg). Two amendments were made to the main study after its freeze, one of them after the runs. Both are documented with their evidence and re-freezes (@sec:prereg). The second corrected a defect that had *inflated* the baselines' failure rates; the verdicts of H1–H4 and H6 held before and after it, and H5's cost criterion for `gpt-5.4-nano` moved from inconclusive to confirmed. The restriction of H6 to hard worlds was decided on pilot data before the freeze, but it is a choice a reader may weigh.

**Step mapping and grading.** For fault injection and grading, language-model writes are mapped to plan steps by their key fields; a rephrased write that escapes the mapping is treated as a new intent, and its effects are still graded. Grading uses service-side records only. Mail cannot be deleted, so a duplicated e-mail is always counted. For Gitea deletions I rely on a compensation log written by the harness, because deleted issues and comments are no longer visible through Gitea's API.

**Tags: visibility and forgery.** In my stack the tag of an issue or comment is a visible body line. Real deployments can often place it in a hidden field instead; where only visible fields exist, the tag is a cost in presentation that some teams may not accept. A visible tag can also be copied into another record, for instance when someone quotes the original. Settle would then count the copy as its own, skip a needed write or compensate another user's record. Requiring the tag together with the client's own author account would close most of this gap; my implementation checks the tag only.

**What reversal leaves behind.** Deleting a surplus issue or row restores the state my grader checks, but creating it may already have sent notifications or triggered webhooks, and another user may have commented on the duplicate before it was removed. The grader does not count notifications, so reversible in my sense means reversible in the service's state, not in its side effects. Interactions are a real hazard: all of rollback's collateral damage arose because it deleted the agent's own issue together with the other user's comment on it.

**Implementation gaps.** In the main study, three behaviours of the implementation differed from the design that the theory supports: Settle resolved an ambiguous content-only write by content, its final sweep waited only after visible trouble, and in the language-model runs it bound writes to intents through the harness's plan mapping. The follow-up measured the design without these gaps (S2, S−np): it reports every content-only failure, catches delayed redelivery of reversible writes at a cost of about 20 s per episode, and gives identical language-model outcomes without the plan mapping in every ambiguous world. Two gaps remain untested. Compensations ran on a fault-free path, so their own ambiguity was not exercised, and a visible tag could be copied into another record.

**Design of the fault grid.** The pooled rates depend on the mix of modes I chose. Two modes, redelivery and partial batches, are failed by construction by every condition without a final sweep or row-level checks, and @tbl:h1modes therefore also reports the comparison without them. The crash pair penalises only eager re-issuing, because the driver's checkpoint re-issues a step that crashed before dispatch in every condition.

# Conclusion {#sec:conclusion}

An agent that loses the reply to a write does not know whether the action happened, and no amount of reasoning recovers what the observation does not contain. What recovers it is attribution: the ability to count the effects of one's own operation. I defined attributability as identifiability and derived which guarantee each API contract and read model admits. Prevention needs a durable key unless the in-flight bound is known and waited out, and a key protects only within its retention. Convergence needs only attribution, reversibility and an in-flight bound. A policy that must stop at a horizon has to bet between two indistinguishable worlds. And abstention is the honest answer everywhere else. A census of 52 APIs shows that keys are the exception outside payments, while a taggable field is the rule. Settle implements the map with a journal written before dispatch, a tag in a field the API already persists, a reconciliation horizon and compensation gated on evidence. On real services with real faults and under a frozen protocol, Settle converged where the theory says convergence is possible. Its remaining failures were violations of its assumed in-flight bound, whose risk trades against latency, or lay where the theory admits no guarantee. It reported every abstention on a shared aggregate and every unrecoverable duplicate, and in its designed form also every content-only failure and every late-arriving duplicate of an e-mail. An internally pre-registered follow-up located the boundaries of the method more precisely than the main study could. A tag-based prevention policy is as good as convergence on ordinary faults, so convergence is needed mainly for transport duplicates. An author filter combined with waiting is as good as a tag when other writers use other accounts, so the tag is needed for same-account look-alikes and services without authorship. And a frontier model fails without the layer in the same places as smaller ones, while with it all four models converge alike. Keys and workflow engines remain the right tool when the service honours a key. For the many services that do not, the question an agent should ask is not "did my request arrive?" but "how many of my effects exist?"
