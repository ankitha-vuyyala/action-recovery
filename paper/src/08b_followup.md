# An internally pre-registered follow-up study {#sec:followup}

The main study left six questions open that only new experiments could settle. Was a simpler tag-based *prevention* policy as good as Settle? Did Settle's implementation gaps (@sec:limits) matter? Would a duplicate that the network delivers late escape it? Do look-alikes from another account defeat content checks as easily as same-account look-alikes do? Does the language-model result depend on the harness's plan mapping? And would a frontier model change it? I answered them in a follow-up study with its own analysis plan, hypotheses F1–F7 and pass criteria, frozen before the first follow-up episode (`docs/07_followup_plan.md`; freeze digest prefix `87ddadb5`). The main study's records and verdicts are unchanged. The follow-up ran {{fu.n.all|int}} episodes with no exclusions: {{fu.n.scripted|int}} scripted episodes, {{fu.n.np|int}} episodes with the three original models and {{fu.n.sol|int}} with `gpt-5.6-sol`. It cost {{fu.usd.new|usd}} in model calls, under a hard cap of US$ 9.90. All seven hypotheses were confirmed (@tbl:followup). One analysis defect was corrected after the runs: the frozen script treated a rate of exactly zero as missing, so it reported the two F4 criteria on S2 as not confirmed although S2's measured rates (0 of 51 and 0 of 21) met them. Every other verdict is the same under the frozen and the corrected script; both are released (@sec:prereg). Several follow-up hypotheses check properties that follow from the conditions' definitions, such as that a policy without a final sweep misses redelivery; F1(a), F5 and F7 are the informative ones.

**Prevention versus convergence (F1).** The new condition Sp is the policy that the corrected Proposition 3 recommends for every write. It keeps the durable journal and the tag, but after an ambiguous outcome it waits out the in-flight bound, counts by tag and re-issues only if no effect exists. It never compensates and has no final sweep. On the H1 population without redelivery and heavy-tailed delays, Sp and Settle were *identical*: neither left any of the {{fu.f1.eq.pairs|int}} paired worlds inconsistent. The two differ only where the theory says they must. On transport redelivery Sp was inconsistent in {{fu.f1.red.Sp.k|int}} of {{fu.f1.red.Sp.n|int}} worlds and Settle in {{fu.f1.red.S.k|int}}, because preventing one's own re-issues does nothing about a duplicate the transport creates. Under heavy-tailed delays Sp was worse by {{fu.f1.lt.diff|pp}} ({{fu.f1.lt.Sp|pct}} against {{fu.f1.lt.S|pct}}), but this rests on {{fu.f1.lt.Sp_only|int}} discordant worlds (exact McNemar $p$ = {{fu.f1.lt.p|f3}}). In all of them the commit landed between 21.0 and 25.6 s, just past the 20 s bound: Sp's last decision comes $\Delta$ after the attempt and Settle's at the end of the episode, a few seconds later (Proposition 7). The difference is therefore a matter of decision timing, which a slightly larger bound for Sp would remove, not a robust advantage of convergence. In exchange, Sp finished the episodes of the H1 population about 3 s sooner on average (paired difference {{fu.f1.lat.diff|s}}, 95% CI {{fu.f1.lat.lo|f1}} to {{fu.f1.lat.hi|f1}} s). Over the whole H1 population Sp was inconsistent in {{fu.h1.Sp|pct}} of worlds against Settle's {{h1.S.rate|pct}}, and {{fu.f1.red.Sp.k|int}} of its {{fu.h1.Sp.k|int}} failures were redeliveries. The comparison settles what Settle's extra machinery buys, and it is narrower than the headline comparison suggests. When the failure is a lost reply, a request that never arrived, a crash, a partial batch or a bounded delay, a tag-based wait-then-count policy is exactly as good. Convergence matters for duplicates that the transport creates.

**Settle as designed (F2, F3).** The variant S2 closes the first two implementation gaps. It never confirms an ambiguous content-only write by a content match, and its final sweep always waits out the bound. On the {{fu.f2.S2.over.n|int}} ambiguous content-only worlds, S2 left the same share inconsistent as Settle ({{fu.f2.S2.inc|pct}}). But it overclaimed in none of them, against {{fu.f2.S.over.k|int}} for Settle, so every content-only failure is now reported. S2's ambiguous content-only writes are reported as uncertain also when they turned out fine, which is the cost of abstaining. On the H1 population S2 and Settle were again identical. The price of always waiting is paid in every episode: S2 adds {{fu.f3.free_time|s}} to a fault-free episode (95% CI {{fu.f3.free_time.lo|f1}} to {{fu.f3.free_time.hi|f1}} s), close to the assumed bound of 20 s. This exceeds the main study's own latency criterion for fault-free episodes (H5: at most 2 s more than the guard), which S2 would therefore fail.

**Delayed redelivery (F4).** In the new mode `redelivery_late`, the write is acknowledged normally, and the transport delivers an identical copy 8 s later, after a Settle episode without ambiguity has already finished. On the {{fu.f4.reversible.S2.n|int}} worlds whose write is reversible and attributable, every condition except S2 ended inconsistent in every world, including Settle and the outcome oracle; S2 ended inconsistent in none. On the {{fu.f4.irreversible.S2.n|int}} worlds whose write is an e-mail, every condition ended inconsistent, as Proposition 3 says it must: no client can recall a delivered duplicate. But S2 reported every one of them, while every other condition claimed success in all of them. On the {{fu.f4.keyed.S2.n|int}} keyed worlds no condition failed, because the service drops the duplicate key. The design choice is therefore real. A sweep that waits only when something looked ambiguous saves about 20 s per clean episode, and misses every duplicate that arrives after the agent has finished. S2 catches only duplicates that arrive within $\Delta + \lambda$ of the last attempt; a redelivery after its horizon is untested and would be missed as well.

**Look-alikes from another account (F5).** In the new read variant `lookalike_other`, a second, ordinary Gitea user creates the look-alike at the moment of the fault. Content checks without an author filter fared as before: verify-before-retry and the waiting guard were each inconsistent in {{fu.f5.C5|pct}} of the {{fu.f5.C5.n|int}} worlds. They failed even in some lost-reply and late-commit worlds (6 of 21 each), in a way the main study already showed for same-account look-alikes: after an issue's reply was lost, they resolved the issue's number by its title, could pick the other user's issue, and the task's comments were then missing from the agent's own issue. With an author filter, the waiting guard (Gwa) was inconsistent in {{fu.f5.Gwa.k|int}} worlds, exactly like Settle. Verify-before-retry with the filter (C5a) still failed every late-commit world ({{fu.f5.mode.late.C5a|pct0}}), because it does not wait. This result limits the scope of Proposition 4 in practice, and it is shown here only for Gitea issues and comments, four fault modes and the scripted driver. The tag is *necessary* when the duplicate-looking record can come from the same account, for instance from another instance of the same agent or another worker using a shared service account, or when the service records no author, as for the mail, rows and stream events of my stack. Where every other writer uses a different account and the service exposes authorship, the client's own account is itself an attribution source in the sense of Definition 1, and an author filter *combined with waiting out the bound* is as good as a tag.

**Plan-free intent identity (F6).** S−np binds each model write to an intent by the tool and the normalised intent fields of the call itself, without the harness's mapping onto plan steps. For all three original models it produced exactly the same outcome as Settle in every hard and every easy world. In hard worlds each model was again at {{fu.f6.gpt-5.6-luna.hard.Snp|pct}}, with no discordant pair. The plan mapping therefore did not contribute to the language-model results. Part of this identity is mechanical: model responses are cached by the request, so where S−np and Settle present a model with the same history it receives the same response, and the two conditions can diverge only after their observations differ. Binding by intent fields does carry the risk that two deliberately identical writes are merged; no task in my suite contains such a pair.

**A frontier model (F7).** With `gpt-5.6-sol` as the agent and no recovery layer, {{fu.f7.hard.C0|pct}} of hard worlds ended inconsistent ({{fu.f7.hard.C0.k|int}} of {{fu.f7.hard.C0.n|int}}), close to `gpt-5.6-luna`'s {{llm.gpt-5.6-luna.C0.hard|pct}}. With plan-free Settle underneath, {{fu.f7.hard.Snp|pct}} did (paired difference {{fu.f7.hard.diff|pp}}, 95% CI {{fu.f7.hard.lo|pp}} to {{fu.f7.hard.hi|pp}}; exact McNemar $p$ {{fu.f7.hard.p|p}}). The frontier model behaved like the careful smaller one. After its first ambiguous write it checked state first in {{fu.rq1.check|pct0}} of episodes and re-issued at once in only {{fu.rq1.reissue|pct0}}. Its failures fell in the same cells: {{fu.f7.cell.lookalike|pct0}} of look-alike worlds, {{fu.f7.cell.crash_after|pct0}} of crash worlds and {{fu.f7.cell.late_tail|pct0}} of heavy-tailed-delay worlds were inconsistent, against {{fu.f7.cell.lost_ack|pct0}} after a lost reply with exact reads. A more capable model does not escape the cells where the observation is insufficient or the framework re-issues before the model acts. Its cost per episode was {{fu.f7.usd.Snp|usd}} with Settle and {{fu.f7.usd.C0|usd}} without. These figures count paid calls only, and the two conditions share cached responses for identical request histories, so they understate both.
